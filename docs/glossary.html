Glossary

Key terms from the courses, in brief, and what to do when something goes wrong

Short explanations of terms that come up again and again. Labels show where a term is introduced: LM = Linear Models in R (with session number), ORES = Open, Replicable, and Ethical Science, TAE = Testing for Absence and Equivalence, WS = short workshops.

Entries marked Problem describe something that can go wrong, with steps towards a solution under What to do about it?

A

AIC (Akaike information criterion)

LM 09

A measure of how well a model predicts new data, balancing fit against the number of parameters. Lower is better. Only differences between models fitted to the same data are meaningful; ΔAIC < 2 means the models are hard to tell apart. See also model selection.

Alpha spending

ORES

In a sequential analysis, the overall type I error rate (α, usually .05) is distributed (“spent”) across the planned interim looks, so that the error rate over all looks stays at α. Common spending functions are Pocock and O’Brien–Fleming.

Assumptions (of the linear model)

LM 02–03

The linear model assumes that (1) observations are independent, (2) the relationship between predictors and response is linear (on the link scale), (3) residuals have constant variance (homoscedasticity) and (4) residuals are normally distributed. Independence matters most, normality least. Check assumptions with residual plots, not with significance tests.

B

Bayes factor

TAE

The ratio of how well two hypotheses predicted the observed data, e.g. BF01 = 5: the data are five times more likely under H0 than under H1. Unlike a p-value, it can quantify evidence for the null. Depends on the prior for the effect under H1, so report and justify it.

C

Centring

LM 03, 05

Subtracting a value (usually the mean, or a meaningful reference such as trial 1) from a predictor. It changes the meaning of the intercept and of main effects in models with interactions, but not the fit of the model. See also z-transformation.

Collinearity

LM 03 Problem

Two or more predictors are strongly correlated, so the model cannot separate their effects. Symptoms: large standard errors, estimates that change a lot when a predictor is added or removed. Diagnose with variance inflation factors (car::vif(), performance::check_collinearity()); VIF > 5–10 is often taken as a warning sign.

What to do about it?

  • Ask whether both predictors are needed for the research question.
  • Combine them (e.g. into an index or a principal component) if they measure the same thing.
  • Accept wide intervals: collinearity is a property of the data, not an error. Do not drop predictors just to make p-values smaller.

Complete separation

LM 10 Problem

In a binomial model, a predictor (or combination) perfectly predicts the 0/1 outcome, e.g. all animals in one condition chose “yes”. The maximum-likelihood estimate is then infinite: huge estimates and standard errors, warnings such as “fitted probabilities numerically 0 or 1 occurred”.

What to do about it?

  • Check the cross-tabulation of predictor and response to find the cause.
  • Use penalised (Firth) logistic regression (logistf, brglm2) or a Bayesian model with a weakly informative prior.
  • Report the pattern descriptively; it may be the most informative result.

Confidence interval

LM 02

A range computed so that, across many repetitions of the study, 95% of such intervals contain the true value. It does not mean that this particular interval contains the true value with 95% probability; that is the interpretation of a Bayesian credible interval.

Contrasts and dummy coding

LM 04

How a categorical predictor is turned into numbers. Dummy (treatment) coding, R’s default, compares each level with a reference level. Sum coding compares each level with the grand mean, which makes main effects interpretable in models with interactions. Set with contrasts(); check what R did with model.matrix().

Convergence failure

LM 11 Problem

The fitting algorithm did not reliably find the maximum of the likelihood, e.g. lme4’s “Model failed to converge with max|grad| = …”. Common in mixed models with complex random effects, few levels of a grouping factor, or predictors on very different scales. The estimates may or may not be trustworthy.

What to do about it?

  1. z-transform or rescale continuous predictors.
  2. Try another optimizer: control = glmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa"), or compare all with allFit(). If they give the same estimates, the warning is usually a false alarm.
  3. Increase the number of iterations, e.g. optCtrl = list(maxfun = 1e5).
  4. Check for a singular fit; simplify the random-effects structure step by step (e.g. drop the correlations first: (1 + x || id)).
  5. Consider glmmTMB or a Bayesian model (brms), where priors stabilise the fit.

Cook’s distance

LM 02

A measure of how much the model’s estimates change when a single observation is removed. Large values flag influential cases. See influential cases.

Credible interval

WS Bayes, TAE

In Bayesian statistics, an interval that contains the parameter with a given probability (e.g. 95%), given the model, the data and the prior.

D

Degrees of freedom

LM 02

Roughly, the number of observations minus the number of estimated parameters. They determine the shape of the t- and F-distributions used for tests. In mixed models they are not well defined, which is why lme4 reports no p-values for lmer().

Divergent transitions

WS Bayes Problem

A warning from Stan that the sampler had trouble exploring parts of the posterior. Even a few divergences can bias results.

What to do about it?

  • Increase adapt_delta (e.g. control = list(adapt_delta = 0.95)).
  • Use more informative priors, especially on standard deviations.
  • Simplify or reparameterise the model; check for weakly identified parameters.

Dummy coding

See contrasts and dummy coding.

E

Effect size

LM 02

How large an effect is, in units that matter: a raw difference (e.g. grams, seconds), a standardised difference (Cohen’s d), an odds ratio, or a change in probability. Report it with a confidence interval; a p-value alone says nothing about size.

Equivalence test (TOST)

TAE

Tests whether an effect is smaller than the smallest effect size of interest. Two one-sided tests check whether the effect is significantly above −SESOI and significantly below +SESOI. If both are significant, the effect is “statistically equivalent” to zero.

Estimated marginal means

LM 04

Model-based means for each level of a factor (or combination of factors), averaged over the other predictors. Computed with emmeans; the basis for pairwise comparisons and for reporting results on the response scale.

F

Failure to converge

See convergence failure.

Fixed effect

LM 11

A model parameter that is the same for all units, e.g. the average effect of age. In mixed models, contrasted with random effects, which vary between units.

Full–null model comparison

LM 09

Comparing the full model (with the predictors of interest) with a null model that lacks them but keeps all control predictors and random effects. A likelihood-ratio test of the two protects against cryptic multiple testing when there are several test predictors.

H

Heteroscedasticity

LM 02–03 Problem

The residual variance is not constant, e.g. it increases with the fitted values (a “funnel” in the residual plot). Standard errors and p-values can then be wrong.

What to do about it?

  • Ask whether the response type calls for a GLM (counts and proportions are heteroscedastic by nature).
  • Transform the response (e.g. log) if this is meaningful.
  • Model the variance explicitly (glmmTMB with dispformula, nlme::gls), or use robust standard errors (sandwich).

I

Influential cases

LM 02 Problem

Single observations that strongly change the results (see Cook’s distance), often outliers with unusual predictor values.

What to do about it?

  • Check for data errors first.
  • Fit the model with and without these cases and report both if conclusions differ.
  • Do not delete cases just because they are inconvenient; decide on exclusion rules in advance (see preregistration).

Interaction

LM 05

The effect of one predictor depends on the value of another, e.g. age affects foraging differently in two populations. In R: y ~ a * b. With an interaction in the model, the “main effects” are conditional effects at the zero point of the other predictor, so centring matters.

Intercept

LM 02

The predicted response when all predictors are zero (or at their reference level). Only meaningful if zero is a meaningful value, which is one reason for centring.

L

Likelihood-ratio test

LM 09–11

Compares two nested models by how much the likelihood improves with the extra parameters. In R: anova(m0, m1, test = "Chisq"). The standard way to test effects in GLMs and GLMMs.

Link function

LM 10

In a GLM, the function that connects the linear predictor to the expected response, e.g. the log link for counts (Poisson) and the logit link for probabilities (binomial). Coefficients are on the link scale; transform them back (e.g. exp(), plogis()) or plot predictions to interpret them.

Log-odds (logit)

LM 10

The scale of binomial models: log(p / (1 − p)). 0 corresponds to p = .5; ±2 to about .12 and .88. A coefficient is the change in log-odds per unit of the predictor; exp() turns it into an odds ratio.

M

Model selection

LM 09

Choosing among candidate models. For confirmatory questions, specify the model in advance and test it (e.g. full–null comparison). For exploratory or predictive questions, compare a small set of plausible models with AIC. Avoid stepwise selection by p-values (see overfitting).

O

Offset

LM 10

A term in a count model whose coefficient is fixed at 1, used when observation time or effort differs between observations. With a log link, offset(log(hours)) turns a model of counts into a model of rates (counts per hour), without estimating an extra parameter.

Optional stopping

ORES Problem

Testing repeatedly as data come in and stopping as soon as p < .05. This inflates the type I error rate far above 5% (see the simulation on the ORES page).

What to do about it?

  • Fix the sample size in advance, based on a power analysis.
  • If you want to look early, plan a sequential analysis with alpha spending.
  • Bayesian analyses are less affected, but their error rates still depend on the stopping rule; plan it.

Overdispersion

LM 10 Problem

The data vary more than the model assumes. A Poisson model assumes variance = mean; real counts usually vary more. Ignoring it gives too small standard errors and too many false positives. Check with the ratio of residual deviance (or Pearson χ²) to residual df (≫ 1 is a warning sign), performance::check_overdispersion() or DHARMa.

What to do about it?

  • Check for missing predictors or non-independence first; overdispersion is often a symptom.
  • Use a negative binomial model (MASS::glm.nb, glmmTMB(family = nbinom2)).
  • In GLMMs, an observation-level random effect, (1 | obs), can absorb extra variation.
  • For many zeros, consider zero-inflation.

Overfitting

LM 09 Problem

The model fits noise in the sample rather than the underlying pattern, usually because it has too many parameters for the amount of data. It fits the sample well but predicts new data poorly, and its effects are overestimated.

What to do about it?

  • Limit the number of predictors (a common rule of thumb: at least 10–20 observations per parameter).
  • Specify the model from the research question, not from the data.
  • Evaluate predictions on new data or with cross-validation.

P

Partial pooling

LM 11

What random effects do: estimates for each individual are pulled towards the overall mean, more strongly for individuals with little data. A compromise between ignoring individuals (complete pooling) and estimating each one separately (no pooling).

Polynomial term

LM 08

A squared (or higher-order) predictor, e.g. y ~ age + I(age^2), to model curved relationships within the linear model. Centre the predictor first to reduce collinearity between the terms, or use poly().

Power

LM 08, WS Power

The probability of detecting an effect of a given size, if it exists. Depends on effect size, sample size, variability and α. For complex models, estimate it by simulation (see power analysis by simulation).

Preregistration

ORES

A time-stamped plan of hypotheses, design, sample size and analysis, written before the data are collected or seen. It separates confirmatory from exploratory analyses. Platforms: OSF, AsPredicted.

Prior, posterior

WS Bayes, TAE

In Bayesian statistics, the prior expresses what is plausible about a parameter before seeing the data; the posterior combines prior and data (via the likelihood). Check what your priors imply with a prior predictive check.

Pseudoreplication

LM 11 Problem

Treating repeated observations of the same unit (animal, group, session) as independent. The effective sample size is overstated, so standard errors are too small and false positives too frequent.

What to do about it?

  • Identify the unit of observation and all grouping factors before the analysis.
  • Include random effects for them, with random slopes for predictors that vary within units.
  • Alternatively, aggregate to one value per unit (loses information but is valid).

p-value

LM 02

The probability of a result at least as extreme as the observed one, if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and p > .05 is not evidence for “no effect” (see equivalence test).

R

Random effects (random intercepts and slopes)

LM 11

Parameters that vary between units of a grouping factor (e.g. chimps), modelled as coming from a common distribution. A random intercept, (1 | id), lets units differ in their baseline; a random slope, (1 + x | id), lets the effect of x differ between units. Random slopes are needed for predictors that vary within units, otherwise tests of these predictors are too liberal.

Reference level

LM 04

With dummy coding, the factor level that the intercept refers to and that all other levels are compared with. R uses the first level alphabetically; change it with relevel() or factor(..., levels = ).

Registered report

ORES

A publication format in which the introduction, methods and analysis plan are peer-reviewed before data collection. If accepted in principle, the paper is published regardless of the results.

Residuals

LM 02

Observed minus predicted values. Plots of residuals against fitted values and predictors are the main tool for checking assumptions. For GLMs and GLMMs, use simulated (quantile) residuals from DHARMa.

Rhat and effective sample size (ESS)

WS Bayes

Convergence diagnostics in Bayesian models. Rhat compares chains; values above 1.01 suggest they have not converged. ESS is the number of effectively independent draws; it should be at least a few hundred per parameter.

ROPE (region of practical equivalence)

TAE

A range of parameter values considered practically equivalent to zero (defined via the SESOI). If (almost) the whole posterior lies inside the ROPE, the effect is accepted as practically zero.

3Rs

ORES

Principles for the ethical use of animals in research: Replacement, Reduction and Refinement. Good statistical planning (power analysis, sequential designs) contributes directly to Reduction.

S

Sequential analysis

ORES

A design with planned interim analyses, at which data collection can stop for efficacy or futility, while alpha spending keeps the overall error rate at α. With animals, it reduces the expected number of animals needed. In R: rpact.

SESOI (smallest effect size of interest)

TAE

The smallest effect that would be theoretically or practically meaningful. Needed for equivalence tests, ROPEs and power analyses. Justify it from theory, previous studies or practical considerations, and preregister it.

Singular fit

LM 11 Problem

lme4’s message “boundary (singular) fit”: a random-effect variance is estimated as (nearly) zero or a correlation as ±1. The random-effects structure is too complex for the data, often because a grouping factor has few levels.

What to do about it?

  • Inspect VarCorr(model) to see which component is at the boundary.
  • Simplify: first remove the random-effect correlations (||), then random slopes with (near-)zero variance, guided by the design rather than by p-values.
  • Keep in mind that removing random slopes for within-unit predictors makes tests of these predictors too liberal; report what you did.
  • A Bayesian model (brms) with weakly informative priors avoids estimates at the boundary.

Standard error

LM 02

The standard deviation of an estimate across repeated samples: how much the estimate would vary if the study were repeated. The basis of confidence intervals and tests.

Stopping for futility

ORES

Ending a study early at a planned interim analysis because the effect is unlikely to reach significance even with the full sample. Saves resources and, in animal research, animals.

Z

z-transformation (standardisation)

LM 03

Subtracting the mean of a predictor and dividing by its standard deviation: scale(x). The predictor then has mean 0 and SD 1, so coefficients are the effect of a one-SD change. Makes effects of predictors on different scales comparable and often helps models to converge.

Zero-inflation

LM 10, WS GLMMs Problem

More zeros than a Poisson or negative binomial model predicts, e.g. because some animals never show a behaviour. Ignoring it can bias estimates. Check with DHARMa::testZeroInflation() or performance::check_zeroinflation().

What to do about it?

  • Ask where the extra zeros come from (e.g. animals that were never in a position to show the behaviour).
  • Fit a zero-inflated or hurdle model with glmmTMB (ziformula = ~ 1); see advanced GLMMs.
  • Sometimes a negative binomial model alone already accounts for the zeros; compare the models.
